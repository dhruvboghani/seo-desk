Internal links are links from one page on your website to another page on the same website. They're easy to overlook, but they do three important jobs:

1. **Help visitors** find related information and the next step, such as from a guide to a product.
2. **Help search engines discover pages.** Crawlers find most pages by following links. A page with no internal links pointing to it, an "orphan page", may never be found.
3. **Show what matters.** Pages that many others link to look important. The words in a link tell search engines what the target page is about.

## Types of internal links

- **Navigation:** the main menu, header and footer. Every page gets these, so use them for your most important pages.
- **Breadcrumbs:** "Home / Sarees / Banarasi" at the top of a page. They show structure and give easy links back up.
- **Contextual links:** links inside the text of a page. These are the most useful for both readers and search engines, because the surrounding text explains the connection.
- **Related content:** "You might also like" sections at the end of posts or products.

## Anchor text

The anchor text is the clickable words. It should describe the page it links to:

- Good: "see our [guide to canonical tags](/blog/canonical-tags-guide)"
- Weak: "to learn more, [click here](/blog/canonical-tags-guide)"

Descriptive anchors help screen reader users too, as they often jump from link to link. Vary the wording naturally. You don't need the exact keyword every time.

## A plan for a small site

### 1. Decide your most important pages

Usually your home page, main service or product category pages and contact page. These should be in the main menu and get the most links.

### 2. Link down from categories, up from details

A category page links to each product or service in it. Each product or service page links back to its category and to closely related items.

### 3. Connect guides to money pages

Every blog post or guide should link to at least one relevant service or product page, where it genuinely helps the reader. A guide on "how to identify pure silk" naturally links to your silk saree collection.

### 4. Link new pages from old ones

When you publish something new, find two or three existing pages on related topics and add a link to the new page. This gets it discovered faster than waiting for the sitemap.

### 5. Fix orphan pages

Check your sitemap against your menus and content. Any page in the sitemap that nothing links to needs at least one link, or should be removed if it isn't useful.

## How many links per page?

There's no fixed number. Add links where they help the reader. A long guide might have ten contextual links, while a short product page might have three. What to avoid is a block of dozens of links that serve no reader, such as a footer listing every city you've ever served.

## Common mistakes

- **Links only in the menu**, with none inside the content.
- **Broken internal links** after renaming or deleting pages. Update links rather than relying on [redirects](/blog/redirects-301-vs-302).
- **Linking to redirected URLs** such as `http://` versions or old addresses.
- **Using images as links without alt text**, which leaves the link with no description.
- **Using `nofollow` on internal links.** It's rarely needed and can stop crawlers following paths you want found.
